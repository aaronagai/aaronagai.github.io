Kuala Lumpur: talking points for an MIT book interview

5 November, 2026

These are the talking points I prepared for YB Puan Hannah Yeoh, Minister in the Prime Minister’s Department (Federal Territories), for an interview with Prof. Siqi Zheng (MIT) and Prof. Matthew E. Kahn (USC) for Triumph and Resilience of Asian Cities.

The answers are written in the Minister’s voice, so “I” means the Minister, not me. One passage has been redacted.

Q1: KL’s growth engine

Looking back over the past three decades, what would you point to as the main engine behind Kuala Lumpur’s growth — trade and services, infrastructure investment, migration, foreign investment? What is the most impressive aspect about how that growth unfolded?

Short answer: infrastructure made the services economy possible, and services created the jobs.

Infrastructure, specifically: places to do business. The Petronas Twin Towers opened in 1999 and turned KLCC into a business district (PETRONAS). Today TRX is being built as an international financial district; HSBC moved its Malaysian headquarters there in 2022 (HSBC Malaysia).

Infrastructure, specifically: connections. KLIA opened in 1998 and handled 63 million passengers in 2025 (MAHB). KL Sentral opened in 2001 as the country’s main transport hub, linking the airport train, intercity trains and city rail (MRCB).

How that grew services: offices gave banks, traders and professional firms a place to cluster. The airport and rail links connected them to the region, and to workers coming in from all over the Klang Valley.

The result: services grew from 88.6% of KL’s economy in 2005 to almost 92% in 2025. Manufacturing fell from 5.8% to 2.6% (DOSM).

Jobs followed. More than 1.16 million people work in KL today. The biggest employers are wholesale and retail, food and accommodation, construction, professional services and finance (DOSM, 2025).

And the jobs kept up with the people. From 2020 to 2025, KL added about 160,000 jobs (1,006,400 to 1,166,400). That is why unemployment is only 2.6% of the labour force: people who come to KL find work (DOSM).

Incomes rose almost four times. Median household income went from RM2,828 a month (1999) to RM10,802 (2024). The average went from about RM4,100 to almost RM14,000 (DOSM).

Most impressive: the city kept making room for people. People came from all over Malaysia, found work and built a life.

My measure: buildings are only worth it if they give ordinary people a fair chance to move up.

Q2: The next 20 years

Looking twenty years ahead, do you expect the same forces to keep driving Kuala Lumpur’s growth, or is the city shifting toward a different model?

The engine stays, the model changes. Building outwards, more land and more construction will not carry KL for the next 20 years.

We are near full employment. Unemployment is 2.64% of the labour force (DOSM, 2025). Growth must come from better jobs, not just more workers.

We have run out of land. KL cannot keep spreading out. We must make better use of the city we already have.

Population growth has slowed from about 1.7% a year (2000 to 2019) to 0.3% in 2026 (DOSM).

The plan points the same way. The Kuala Lumpur Local Plan 2040 targets up to 1.2 million jobs, 305,000 affordable homes and at least 20 square metres of open space per resident (New Straits Times).

What will attract people: clean streets, safe parks, no floods and good homes. Talent will choose KL because it is a good place to raise a family, not because of another tower.

Q3: Environment and competitiveness

Has environmental quality — cleaner air, better water management, greener public space, energy use and carbon emission, and so on — become something that makes Kuala Lumpur more competitive in attracting talent, businesses, and investment, or is it still seen mainly as a cost?

It is not a cost. It is a reason people choose a city. Talent asks: is the air clean, is there a park for my children, is it safe to walk?

Floods hit the economy. When a main road floods, shops close, workers can’t get to work and deliveries stop. The 2021 floods cost Malaysia RM6.1 billion (DOSM).

The problem before: many of KL’s parks and open spaces were never gazetted. When green and open spaces are not gazetted, they can be alienated and converted for other purposes (The Rakyat Post). That is how we lost most of the Jinjang and Batu flood retention land (see Q5).

Even gazetted spaces were misused. Some were being used for restaurants, car parks and car showrooms (The Star).

Padang Merbok: the field was fenced off in August 2022, after a protest by lawyers. People could no longer use it (Malay Mail).

16 January 2026: we took down the fences at Padang Merbok and gave the field back to the people (Malay Mail).

Late January 2026: I met residents’ associations and civil society groups (20 and 21 January), and set up a special task force to gazette open and green spaces (The Rakyat Post; The Star).

February 2026: for the first time, the full list of 494 gazetted green and open spaces was published online on the MyHijau portal, so anyone can check (The Rakyat Post).

April to August 2026: we kept gazetting every month: 539 lots by April, 552 lots covering 468 hectares by August, about 655 football fields (The Star).

June 2026: the Agong decreed that the gazetting of green areas must not be delayed (Malay Mail).

July 2026: six months after the task force began, more than 400 parks had been gazetted (The Star).

September 2026: 556 lots gazetted, with more than 200 more in the final stages (Malay Mail).

The rule now: no commercial activity will be approved on gazetted green spaces (Malay Mail). If we don’t lock them in now, we will lose them. With KL land prices, the government will never buy them back (The Star).

Budget 2027 backs this: RM288 million for green spaces, public parks and 22 km of covered walkways in KL, plus RM7 million for inclusive playgrounds, outdoor gyms and fields, so warga emas, children and OKU can use our parks too (MOF).

DBKL’s breakdown: RM200 million for green areas, covering more than 20 new open spaces, upgrades to 12 open spaces and maintenance of six main parks; RM55 million for the 22 km of covered walkways; and RM1 million for buggies at Taman Botani Perdana, so the elderly and OKU can enjoy the gardens too (DBKL).

Bukit Kiara: Budget 2027 gazettes 104 acres of leased land at Bukit Kiara as part of the Federal Park (MOF).

Listening to children. At our Kanak-Kanak Townhall, children told us Dataran Merdeka was boring: there was nothing to do, and with RM10 they could not buy anything (The Star).

From feedback to action: we are now planning activities in our parks for both young and old. Ten major parks now open at 5.30am and stay open until 12.30am on Fridays and Saturdays (The Star; Malay Mail).

Q4: Growth vs environment

In Kuala Lumpur’s experience, where have you seen real tradeoffs between growth and environmental quality, and where have you found ways to advance both at once?

The clearest tradeoff is land. Developers want density, which brings jobs. But if we pave over everything, the city gets hotter and floods more.

The answer is not to stop development. It is to follow the plans and give residents a real say.

Taman Rimba Kiara (TTDI): In July 2017, DBKL approved eight condominium blocks of 45 to 51 storeys on part of the park (Thomas Philip Advocates & Solicitors).

Residents went to court. They lost in the High Court in 2018 but won at the Court of Appeal in January 2021.

The court found DBKL gave no reasons, did not properly consider the binding KL Structure Plan, which marks the land as a city park, and had a conflict of interest (Thomas Philip Advocates & Solicitors).

Doing both at once: 98 longhouse families on that land had waited more than 44 years for homes. In June 2026, the Prime Minister broke ground on permanent homes for all of them, free of charge. Development is limited to 2.33 hectares; the rest will be gazetted as public open space (Malay Mail).

Bukit Kiara buffer: after TTDI residents raised concerns, DBKL set a 300-metre buffer between a proposed development and the federal park. A rare firefly, believed not recorded for more than a century, has been found there again (Malay Mail).

Flood storage under parks: Padang Merbok’s underground tank will hold 12,000 cubic metres (RM11 million) and the field stays open. Perdana Botanical Gardens holds about 23,000 cubic metres (The Star).

Q5: Flood retention ponds

You’ve taken a strong position on protecting Kuala Lumpur’s water retention ponds from development pressure. How did that initiative come about, and how do you weigh the economic value of the land against the city’s need for flood resilience?

19 June 1998: Cabinet decided that land in Jinjang and Batu should be kept for flood retention ponds (Malay Mail).

1998 to 2016: the land was never gazetted. The decision stayed on paper for 18 years (Malay Mail).

October 2016: the Kuala Lumpur Federal Territory Land Executive Committee revoked the reservation (Malay Mail).

The loss: 17 lots, about 80 acres, were alienated to developers for RM214 million in premiums. The Auditor-General’s Report 2019 had flagged six ponds with alienation approvals (Malay Mail).

2026: the MACC found no offence, because the land was never gazetted. I asked the MACC for a full briefing so we understand what happened (Malay Mail).

The impact: if fully developed, the ponds keep only about 30% of their capacity. Less space for rainwater means it floods the city (The Rakyat Post; Malay Mail).

What I did: Froze development approvals on the remaining retention pond land (Malay Mail).

Sped up the gazetting of the remaining open spaces and retention ponds (Malay Mail).

No more development on retention ponds. Developers must meet every JPS condition before building, not after (The Rakyat Post).

KL has no land left, so I am working with the Selangor government on ponds upstream (Malay Mail).

Went underground where possible: Padang Merbok and Perdana Botanical Gardens (The Star).

Budget 2027: RM100 million for Klang Valley flood mitigation, covering retention ponds and river maintenance, plus an assessment of Batu Dam and Klang Gate Dam for the next 30 years (MOF).

How I weigh it: RM214 million sounds like a lot. But the 2021 floods cost Malaysia RM6.1 billion, including RM1.6 billion in damage to homes (DOSM). A retention pond is cheap insurance. It is non-negotiable.

Q6: Climate adaptation

Beyond the retention ponds, what has Kuala Lumpur found most effective in adapting to climate risks (flood, air pollution, drought, landslides, heatwaves, etc.), and what trade-offs have been hardest to manage?

What works: consistency. Fix the worst spots one by one, and keep drains and rivers clean.

Flash flood hotspots fell from 37 (2020) to 14 (2026), a 62% reduction. Credit to former minister Dr Zaliha Mustafa and former mayor Datuk Seri Maimunah Mohd Sharif for regular waterway cleaning (The Star; The Rakyat Post).

2026 DBKL budget: RM84.6 million for flood mitigation and RM32.18 million for slope maintenance (The Star).

On the ground: flood walls in Segambut, Sungai Toba cleared every two months, and teams out the moment it rains (The Star; Free Malaysia Today; The Rakyat Post).

Budget 2027 adds: RM10.6 million for a landslide early warning system at 50 high-risk slopes in KL (MOF), so families living near them are warned early.

RM55 million for quick-win road patching and drain repairs in all 11 KL parliamentary seats (MOF).

RM100 million for Klang Valley retention ponds and river maintenance (MOF).

The hardest tradeoff is land. KL has had more than 3,000 mm of rain a year since 2019, compared to 2,500 to 2,900 mm before. Older drains were not designed for this (Malay Mail).

Private rights vs public safety. The natural answer is more ponds and green space. But landowners have paid premiums and hold titles, and we respect existing titles (Malay Mail; The Rakyat Post).

So we: set flood conditions before approval, build storage underground and work with Selangor, because floods do not stop at the city border.

Q7: Accountability and MP oversight

Our prior research on Chinese cities found that when mayors are appointed rather than elected, they lack the direct electoral incentive that officials in fully elected systems — such as most US cities — have to address pollution, flooding, and other quality-of-life concerns, though rising information transparency and social media have opened up alternative channels of pressure on appointed officials. Kuala Lumpur offers an interesting hybrid case: Malaysia is a competitive multi-party democracy, yet the capital’s own city government has historically operated without directly elected local officials. Against that backdrop, what motivated you to introduce mechanisms like the MP-led oversight committees over DBKL’s spending, and what have you learned so far about how well they work as a source of accountability for issues like flooding and public spending?

KL is a hybrid. Local elections were suspended in the 1960s. DBKL is run by appointed officials, and under the Federal Capital Act 1960 the city is administered by the mayor (Malay Mail; IIUM).

Our commitment: Pakatan Harapan’s 2018 manifesto promised to make local authorities more accountable to residents and to give civil society a direct voice (Pakatan Harapan Manifesto 2018).

The UIA study (Kajian UIA): Began on 1 December 2025, the month I took office, after a Private Member’s Bill on KL mayoral elections was tabled in Parliament (New Straits Times; The Star).

Ran from December 2025 to March 2026, with KL MPs and DBKL management (IIUM).

Found most problems come from weak governance and unclear procedures, not gaps in the law (IIUM).

Found councillors with executive powers now would create overlapping roles and unclear accountability (New Straits Times).

Recommended: fix governance first, strengthen checks and balances, improve DBKL’s Advisory Board, and give MPs a stronger oversight role without administrative powers (IIUM; New Straits Times).

Cabinet decision (July 2026): reform DBKL from within first, in stages, before any change to the law (New Straits Times).

Why it was urgent: the 2025 Local Authority Star Rating showed DBKL had clear room to improve on integrity. So DBKL carried out 16 governance reforms in six months (Malay Mail; The Edge).

The reforms: Five MP-led monitoring committees since January 2026. The flood, roads and slopes committee alone watches RM355.79 million (Malay Mail; The Star).

Special OSC abolished, to keep politics out of planning. Budget 2027 confirms it (MOF).

OSC portal open to the public; every KL MP can see applications and send views to the mayor before approval (Malay Mail).

Mayor no longer chairs the audit committee. Before this, he was auditing his own spending (The Sun).

Mayor can approve contributions only up to RM3,000. Anything above goes to the top management committee (Malay Mail).

Job rotation for sensitive posts; body cameras for enforcement officers (Malay Mail).

Note of Collaboration with the MACC, work with the Malaysia Competition Commission on tender cartels, MoU with the Malaysian Institute of Integrity (The Star; Malay Mail; Institut Integriti Malaysia).

Budget 2027: RM2 million for DBKL to pilot the Malaysia Governance Index with the MACC (MOF).

What I have learned: monitoring works when it is regular and specific. MPs bring residents’ problems straight to the table. Works approved early in the year must be delivered faster. When it is the people’s money, the people deserve to see where it goes (The Star; Harapan Daily).

Q8: Highest-return investment

Among transportation, housing, environment, and others, which types of infrastructure investment do you believe deliver the highest return for Kuala Lumpur residents, and how do you weigh them against each other?

My test: does it protect what people already have, and does it help those who need it most?

1. Flood mitigation, the highest return. One flood can wipe out a family’s savings or a small business in an afternoon. DBKL 2026: RM84.6 million for flood mitigation, RM239.01 million for roads, RM32.18 million for slopes (The Star).

2. Public housing. Old PPR and public flats with broken lifts and leaking pipes hurt the elderly, OKU and mothers with young children most. RM300 million in 2026 to upgrade all 64 public housing projects; 25 done by end-July. At PPR Batu Muda alone, 21 lifts have been replaced (The Edge; Malay Mail).

3. Green spaces. KL already has a lot of infrastructure. What families need now are places to spend time together. Parks also hold rainwater and cool the city (Malay Mail).

Budget 2027 follows the same priorities. RM458.5 million goes through DBKL in 2027 (DBKL):

RM1 billion for at least 2,500 Rumah MADANI homes on government land in Belfield (MOF).

RM89 million for Lestari Niaga and PA/PPR maintenance (MOF): RM66 million for hawker centres, kiosks and trading facilities, and RM23 million for PA/PPR maintenance (DBKL).

RM288 million for green spaces, parks and covered walkways (MOF).

RM4.5 million to turn Anjung Kelana, Taman Desa and Pasar Sementara Cheras into elderly day centres, so warga emas can stay active and connected to their community (DBKL).

I don’t weigh them separately. A park can hold floodwater. An upgraded estate comes with better roads and a playground.

And always: this money belongs to the people. It must be spent well, and residents must help take care of what is built (The Edge).

Q9: Newcomers and moving up

Kuala Lumpur continues to draw migrants from across Malaysia and the region — what have you observed about how newcomers find their footing economically, and what remains hardest about moving up?

KL is where people come to build a better life. About 2.07 million people live in KL; around 270,700 are non-citizens, roughly one in eight (DOSM, 2025).

Newcomers find their footing. Hardcore poverty is 0.0% and absolute poverty 1.0% of KL households (DOSM, 2024). Most jobs in KL are in services: shops, restaurants, offices and deliveries.

But moving up is hard. 11.7% of KL households live in relative poverty, earning less than half the median income (DOSM, 2024).

Housing is the biggest pressure.

The ladder is there, but the bottom steps are steeper. An entry-level salary meeting a city rent leaves little to save.

What we are doing: Looking after affordable public housing.

Budget 2027: Komuniti Angkat MADANI (RM50 million) now extended to urban communities, including PPR, with skills courses from universities and TVET institutions (MOF).

Budget 2027: a 50% rent discount throughout 2027 for more than 10,000 small traders renting DBKL premises (MOF).

Cutting fees and red tape so it is cheaper to start something of your own.

The hardest group is still people experiencing homelessness. That is where we are focusing next.

Q10: Small business hurdles

What are the biggest regulatory hurdles to starting or growing a business in Kuala Lumpur for a small business owner, and what reform would make the most difference?

The problem is not ambition. It is paperwork, delays and cost, plus middlemen who charge thousands to “help”.

My view: time is money, and speed is the currency. If you have complied with the rules, the government should not make you repeat the same process (Malay Mail).

DBKL must be a facilitator, not a stumbling block. (BusinessToday)

Three-year licences: since 1 July 2026, renew for two years (5% discount) or three years (10% discount). 8,000 businesses renewed in the first two months (Malay Mail).

Online, no runners: e-Lesen removes the middleman. 170 DBKL services online by July 2026; all applications online by 2030 (Malay Mail; The Sun).

Faster approvals: compliant development applications approved in as little as 21 days (DBKL).

Cheaper sites for traders: DBKL cut hawker site rentals by 50% (Malay Mail). Budget 2027 gives a 50% rent discount throughout 2027 to more than 10,000 small traders renting DBKL premises (MOF).

Ramadan bazaars: site rental cut from RM500 to RM400, including canopy and insurance, across 41 sites. No subletting (Malay Mail).

Deepavali bazaar: stall cost cut 85%, from RM2,626 to a maximum of RM400. Runs 30 days instead of 21, with 172 lots in Brickfields and Masjid India (Malaysia Dateline).

Gyms: licence fee cut 80%, from RM50 to RM10 per square metre, about RM5,000 a year down to RM1,000. KL has the highest obesity rate in the country, at 40.6% (NHMS 2023; Free Malaysia Today).

Running events: I have asked organisers to agree fixed routes with DBKL and the police. In one week, 30 roads were closed for a single run. Events should go on with less disruption to residents (Malay Mail).

Q11: Housing affordability

Kuala Lumpur has both significant new development and pockets of informal or lower-income housing. How do you think about balancing affordability for lower-income residents against the pressures of rising land value and redevelopment, and what has your ministry found most effective at narrowing housing inequality across the city?

If land is left entirely to the market, lower- and middle-income families get pushed out of the city.

Inequality has come down. KL’s Gini coefficient fell from 0.467 (2004) to 0.375 (2024), below the national 0.390 (DOSM).

But homes are still hard to afford. On DOSM’s scale, 3.0 or below is affordable. KL overall is 4.0; a terrace house is 6.1. Low-cost flats (1.4) and flats (1.8) are still within reach (DOSM, 2024).

So first, protect what we have.

PPR upgrades: RM300 million in 2026 for all 64 PA and PPR projects: repainting, rewiring, generators, fire safety pipes, lifts, roads and playgrounds. 25 done by end-July. PPR Batu Muda alone: RM28.12 million for more than 10,000 residents (The Edge; Harapan Daily; Malay Mail).

Rent owed: public housing rent is only RM124 a month, but residents owe more than RM14 million in rent and RM12 million in water bills across 51 schemes. That is not fair to those who pay on time (Utusan Malaysia).

Demand: 3,000 households are on the DBKL public housing waiting list. Some units go to police and firefighters so enforcement lives in the community (The Edge).

Bukit Kiara: 98 longhouse families who waited more than 44 years get permanent homes, free of charge (Malay Mail).

New homes: the Kuala Lumpur Local Plan 2040 targets 305,000 affordable homes by 2040 (New Straits Times).

Budget 2027: RM1 billion for at least 2,500 Rumah MADANI homes on government land in Belfield (MOF).

Bandar Madani Bukit Jalil as a people’s housing area, complete with a vertical school (MOF).

RM89 million for Lestari Niaga and PA/PPR maintenance (MOF). Of this, RM23 million goes to PA/PPR maintenance: painting, new cables and main switchboards, fire safety systems and water pumps (DBKL).

Q12: KL vs peer cities

How does Kuala Lumpur compare to peer cities like Singapore, Jakarta, or Bangkok on livability and competitiveness — where is it ahead, and where is it behind?

KL is in a strong position, and the rankings show it.

Oxford Economics Global Cities Index 2026 (1,000 cities): KL rose 14 places to 65th, overtaking Hong Kong and Shenzhen. Oxford Economics called KL a “city to watch” in Asia-Pacific and “Malaysia’s leading digital hub” (The Rakyat Post; Oxford Economics).

Ahead of our neighbours: in the 2024 edition, KL (135th) was well ahead of Bangkok (192nd) and Jakarta (284th) (InvestKL).

Our people are our strength: KL ranked 21st in the world for human capital (Oxford Economics, 2024).

Next: concerts, entertainment and events. They fill hotels and restaurants and create jobs for young people.

Proof it pays: the Rain Rave water music festival over the Labour Day weekend brought in RM320 million, with more than 100,000 international visitors spending about RM255 million (DOSM, via Malay Mail).

Cutting red tape for events: Lower DBKL deposits from 1 May 2026: foreign concerts from RM30,000 to RM15,000; foreign stage and cultural shows from RM30,000 to RM8,000; local stage and cultural shows from RM10,000 to RM4,000 (Malay Mail).

KL Headline Season 2026, launched in May, to make KL one of Asia’s leading concert destinations (Malay Mail).

I am personally monitoring approval timelines with the mayor (Malay Mail).

Licensing reform helps the businesses around events too: three-year licences, online applications, 50% rent cuts for traders.

Budget 2027 adds culture: RM32.9 million for a KL Heritage Trail and RM0.5 million for the Kampong Bharu Arts and Heritage Festival (MOF).

My aim: when an artist or organiser thinks of Southeast Asia, KL should be the easiest yes.

Q13: Technology and AI

How is Kuala Lumpur using new technology or AI tools in city management — for example in traffic, flood monitoring, or public spending oversight — and where’s the biggest gap between what’s possible and what’s in place today?

10,000 CCTV cameras at main roads, junctions, public areas and commercial centres, all linked to one AI system at the KL Command and Control Centre, run by DBKL with the police (The Star).

What the system does: Facial recognition to locate suspects while they are still in KL.

Geo-fencing to track a suspect’s vehicle.

Behaviour analysis that flags suspicious movement and alerts the command centre automatically.

Early detection of accidents, emergencies, traffic jams and flood risks.

Public address speakers at KLCC, Bukit Bintang, the Sultan Abdul Samad Building and Jalan Tuanku Abdul Rahman, with Dataran Merdeka next. At KLCC, warnings play every 10 minutes in five languages.

Cameras only in public areas; data kept for 60 days (The Star; The Rakyat Post).

Results: snatch theft down 57.6% in 2025; police efficiency up about 50%; about 50 cases solved with the cameras’ help since 2020. RM500 million invested since 2020 (The Rakyat Post; New Straits Times).

Going digital: 170 DBKL services online by July 2026, all applications online by 2030, body cameras for enforcement officers (Malay Mail).

Budget 2027 adds: RM1.9 million for a Hybrid Command Centre to monitor land, green areas and public assets, and RM10.6 million for a landslide early warning system at 50 slopes (MOF).

The biggest gap: connecting the data. JPS has flood data, DBKL has traffic and planning data, transport operators have commuter data. They are still islands.

What’s possible: one platform to redirect traffic before a storm, send teams before a flood, and show residents how their money is spent. Using the cameras for traffic management is still being discussed with the Transport Ministry (The Star).

Q14: Biggest misconception

What’s the biggest misconception outsiders — investors, researchers, visitors — have about how Kuala Lumpur actually works?

The misconception: KL is a concrete jungle, run from the top down. The reality is different.

KL is greener than people think. 556 lots of open space gazetted, 468 hectares by August 2026, about 655 football fields (Malay Mail; The Star). Budget 2027 adds 104 acres at Bukit Kiara to the Federal Park (MOF).

Nature in the middle of the capital: a rare firefly, believed not recorded for more than a century, was found again at Bukit Kiara (Malay Mail).

Residents have a real voice. There is no elected council, but TTDI residents took DBKL to court over Taman Rimba Kiara and won. MPs now sit on committees that watch DBKL’s spending (Thomas Philip Advocates & Solicitors; Malay Mail).

We listen, even to children. When they said Dataran Merdeka was boring, we started planning activities for them (The Star).

A rich city on average can still leave people behind. Median household income is RM10,802, but 11.7% of households live in relative poverty and 3,000 households are waiting for public housing (DOSM; The Edge).

KL is not defined by its skyline. It is defined by its people, and the parks, homes and voice we protect for them.
