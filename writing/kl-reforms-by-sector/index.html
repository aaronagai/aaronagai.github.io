Reforms by sector

Kuala Lumpur: reforms by sector

5 November, 2026

These are the talking points I prepared for YB Puan Hannah Yeoh, Minister in the Prime Minister’s Department (Federal Territories), for an interview with Prof. Siqi Zheng (MIT) and Prof. Matthew E. Kahn (USC) for Triumph and Resilience of Asian Cities.

The points are written in the Minister’s voice, so “I” means the Minister, not me.

Economy and growth

What built KL: infrastructure made the services economy possible, and services created the jobs.

Places to do business: the Petronas Twin Towers opened in 1999 and turned KLCC into a business district (PETRONAS). TRX is being built as an international financial district; HSBC moved its Malaysian headquarters there in 2022 (HSBC Malaysia).

Connections: KLIA opened in 1998 and handled 63 million passengers in 2025 (MAHB). KL Sentral opened in 2001 as the country’s main transport hub (MRCB).

People: KL is where people come to build a better life. About 2.07 million people live in KL; around 270,700 are non-citizens, roughly one in eight (DOSM, 2025).

Economy: services grew from 88.6% of KL’s economy in 2005 to almost 92% in 2025. Manufacturing fell from 5.8% to 2.6% (DOSM).

Jobs: more than 1.16 million people work in KL. From 2020 to 2025, KL added about 160,000 jobs, and unemployment is only 2.6% of the labour force (DOSM).

Incomes: median household income rose almost four times, from RM2,828 a month (1999) to RM10,802 (2024). The average went from about RM4,100 to almost RM14,000 (DOSM).

Poverty: hardcore poverty is 0.0% and absolute poverty 1.0% of KL households (DOSM, 2024).

Inequality: KL’s Gini coefficient fell from 0.467 (2004) to 0.375 (2024), below the national 0.390 (DOSM). But 11.7% of KL households still live in relative poverty (DOSM, 2024).

Standing: KL rose 14 places to 65th in the Oxford Economics Global Cities Index 2026 (1,000 cities), overtaking Hong Kong and Shenzhen, and was called a “city to watch” in Asia-Pacific (The Rakyat Post; Oxford Economics). In the 2024 edition, KL (135th) was well ahead of Bangkok (192nd) and Jakarta (284th) (InvestKL), and ranked 21st in the world for human capital (Oxford Economics).

The next 20 years: the engine stays, the model changes. Population growth has slowed from about 1.7% a year (2000 to 2019) to 0.3% in 2026 (DOSM), and KL has run out of land. Growth must come from better jobs and better use of the city we already have.

The plan: the Kuala Lumpur Local Plan 2040 targets up to 1.2 million jobs, 305,000 affordable homes and at least 20 square metres of open space per resident (New Straits Times).

What will attract talent: clean streets, safe parks, no floods and good homes. People will choose KL because it is a good place to raise a family, not because of another tower.

Budget 2027: RM458.5 million goes through DBKL in 2027 (DBKL).

Governance and accountability

The starting point: local elections were suspended in the 1960s. DBKL is run by appointed officials, and the city is administered by the mayor (Malay Mail; IIUM).

The UIA study: began on 1 December 2025 and ran until March 2026, with KL MPs and DBKL management. It found most problems come from weak governance and unclear procedures, not gaps in the law (IIUM).

Cabinet decision (July 2026): reform DBKL from within first, in stages, before any change to the law (New Straits Times).

16 governance reforms in six months. The 2025 Local Authority Star Rating showed DBKL had clear room to improve on integrity (Malay Mail; The Edge).

UIA findings: giving councillors executive powers now would create overlapping roles and unclear accountability. It recommended fixing governance first, improving DBKL’s Advisory Board, and giving MPs a stronger oversight role without administrative powers (IIUM; New Straits Times).

MP oversight: five MP-led monitoring committees since January 2026. The flood, roads and slopes committee alone watches RM355.79 million (Malay Mail; The Star).

Planning: Special OSC abolished, to keep politics out of planning; Budget 2027 confirms it (MOF). The OSC portal is open to the public, and every KL MP can see applications and send views to the mayor before approval (Malay Mail).

Audit: the mayor no longer chairs the audit committee. Before this, he was auditing his own spending (The Sun).

Spending limits: the mayor can approve contributions only up to RM3,000. Anything above goes to the top management committee (Malay Mail).

Integrity: job rotation for sensitive posts and body cameras for enforcement officers (Malay Mail). A Note of Collaboration with the MACC, work with the Malaysia Competition Commission on tender cartels, and an MoU with the Malaysian Institute of Integrity (The Star; Malay Mail; Institut Integriti Malaysia).

Budget 2027: RM2 million for DBKL to pilot the Malaysia Governance Index with the MACC (MOF).

What I have learned: monitoring works when it is regular and specific. When it is the people’s money, the people deserve to see where it goes.

Green and open spaces

The problem before: many of KL’s parks and open spaces were never gazetted, so they could be alienated and converted (The Rakyat Post). Even gazetted spaces were being used for restaurants, car parks and car showrooms (The Star).

Padang Merbok: the field was fenced off in August 2022, after a protest by lawyers. People could no longer use it (Malay Mail).

16 January 2026: we took down the fences at Padang Merbok and gave the field back to the people (Malay Mail).

January 2026: met residents’ associations and civil society groups, and set up a special task force to gazette open and green spaces (The Rakyat Post; The Star).

February 2026: for the first time, the full list of 494 gazetted green and open spaces was published online on the MyHijau portal (The Rakyat Post).

Gazetting every month: 552 lots covering 468 hectares by August 2026, about 655 football fields (The Star). 556 lots by September, with more than 200 more in the final stages (Malay Mail).

June 2026: the Agong decreed that the gazetting of green areas must not be delayed (Malay Mail).

The rule now: no commercial activity will be approved on gazetted green spaces (Malay Mail).

Parks for everyone: ten major parks now open at 5.30am and stay open until 12.30am on Fridays and Saturdays (The Star; Malay Mail). After children told us Dataran Merdeka was boring, we are planning activities for young and old (The Star).

Bukit Kiara: 98 longhouse families who waited more than 44 years get permanent homes, free of charge. Development is limited to 2.33 hectares; the rest will be gazetted as public open space (Malay Mail).

Bukit Kiara buffer: a 300-metre buffer between a proposed development and the Federal Park. A rare firefly, believed not recorded for more than a century, has been found there again (Malay Mail).

Budget 2027: RM288 million for green spaces, public parks and 22 km of covered walkways, plus RM7 million for inclusive playgrounds, outdoor gyms and fields (MOF). 104 acres at Bukit Kiara gazetted as part of the Federal Park (MOF).

DBKL’s breakdown: RM200 million for green areas (more than 20 new open spaces, 12 upgrades, six main parks maintained), RM55 million for covered walkways, and RM1 million for buggies at Taman Botani Perdana, so warga emas and OKU can enjoy the gardens too (DBKL).

Flood and climate resilience

Why it matters: the 2021 floods cost Malaysia RM6.1 billion, including RM1.6 billion in damage to homes (DOSM). KL has had more than 3,000 mm of rain a year since 2019 (Malay Mail).

The retention ponds: in 1998, Cabinet decided land in Jinjang and Batu should be kept for flood retention. It was never gazetted, and in 2016 the reservation was revoked. 17 lots, about 80 acres, were alienated for RM214 million in premiums (Malay Mail).

What we did: froze development approvals on the remaining retention pond land, and sped up gazetting of the remaining ponds and open spaces (Malay Mail).

Conditions first: developers must meet every JPS condition before building, not after (The Rakyat Post).

Beyond the border: KL has no land left, so we are working with the Selangor government on ponds upstream (Malay Mail).

Going underground: Padang Merbok’s underground tank will hold 12,000 cubic metres (RM11 million) and the field stays open. Perdana Botanical Gardens holds about 23,000 cubic metres (The Star).

Cheap insurance: RM214 million sounds like a lot, but one flood can wipe out a family’s savings or a small business in an afternoon. A retention pond is non-negotiable.

Results: flash flood hotspots fell from 37 (2020) to 14 (2026), a 62% reduction, building on the work of former minister Dr Zaliha Mustafa and former mayor Datuk Seri Maimunah Mohd Sharif (The Star; The Rakyat Post).

DBKL 2026: RM84.6 million for flood mitigation and RM32.18 million for slope maintenance; flood walls in Segambut and Sungai Toba cleared every two months (The Star; Free Malaysia Today).

Budget 2027: RM100 million for Klang Valley flood mitigation, including an assessment of Batu Dam and Klang Gate Dam for the next 30 years; RM10.6 million for a landslide early warning system at 50 high-risk slopes; RM55 million for road patching and drain repairs in all 11 KL parliamentary seats (MOF).

Housing

Protect what we have: RM300 million in 2026 to upgrade all 64 PA and PPR projects: repainting, rewiring, generators, fire safety, lifts, roads and playgrounds. 25 done by end-July (The Edge; Malay Mail).

PPR Batu Muda: RM28.12 million for more than 10,000 residents; 21 lifts replaced, because broken lifts hurt warga emas, OKU and mothers with young children most (Harapan Daily; Malay Mail).

Fairness: public housing rent is only RM124 a month, but residents owe more than RM14 million in rent and RM12 million in water bills across 51 schemes. That is not fair to those who pay on time (Utusan Malaysia).

Demand: 3,000 households are on the DBKL public housing waiting list. Some units go to police and firefighters so enforcement lives in the community (The Edge).

New homes: the Kuala Lumpur Local Plan 2040 targets 305,000 affordable homes (New Straits Times).

Budget 2027: RM1 billion for at least 2,500 Rumah MADANI homes on government land in Belfield, and Bandar Madani Bukit Jalil as a people’s housing area with a vertical school (MOF). RM23 million for PA/PPR maintenance (DBKL).

Small business and licensing

My view: time is money, and speed is the currency. DBKL must be a facilitator, not a stumbling block (Malay Mail; BusinessToday).

Three-year licences: since 1 July 2026, renew for two years (5% discount) or three years (10% discount). 8,000 businesses renewed in the first two months (Malay Mail).

No more runners: e-Lesen removes the middleman. 170 DBKL services online by July 2026; all applications online by 2030 (Malay Mail; The Sun).

Faster approvals: compliant development applications approved in as little as 21 days (DBKL).

Cheaper sites: hawker site rentals cut by 50% (Malay Mail). Budget 2027: a 50% rent discount throughout 2027 for more than 10,000 small traders renting DBKL premises (MOF), and RM66 million for hawker centres, kiosks and trading facilities (DBKL).

Bazaars: Ramadan bazaar rental cut from RM500 to RM400 across 41 sites (Malay Mail). Deepavali bazaar stall cost cut 85%, from RM2,626 to a maximum of RM400 (Malaysia Dateline).

Gyms: licence fee cut 80%, from RM50 to RM10 per square metre. KL has the highest obesity rate in the country, at 40.6% (NHMS 2023; Free Malaysia Today).

Events, culture and tourism

Lower deposits from 1 May 2026: foreign concerts from RM30,000 to RM15,000; foreign stage and cultural shows from RM30,000 to RM8,000; local shows from RM10,000 to RM4,000 (Malay Mail).

KL Headline Season 2026, launched in May, to make KL one of Asia’s leading concert destinations. I am personally monitoring approval timelines with the mayor (Malay Mail).

Proof it pays: the Rain Rave festival over the Labour Day weekend brought in RM320 million, with more than 100,000 international visitors spending about RM255 million (DOSM, via Malay Mail).

Why events: concerts and events fill hotels and restaurants and create jobs for young people. When an artist or organiser thinks of Southeast Asia, KL should be the easiest yes.

Running events: organisers must agree fixed routes with DBKL and the police, so events go on with less disruption to residents (Malay Mail).

Budget 2027: RM32.9 million for a KL Heritage Trail and RM0.5 million for the Kampong Bharu Arts and Heritage Festival (MOF).

Technology and public safety

One system: 10,000 CCTV cameras at main roads, junctions and public areas, linked to one AI system at the KL Command and Control Centre, run by DBKL with the police (The Star).

What it does: helps locate suspects, flags suspicious movement, and detects accidents, traffic jams and flood risks early. Cameras are only in public areas, and data is kept for 60 days (The Star; The Rakyat Post).

Public address speakers at KLCC, Bukit Bintang, the Sultan Abdul Samad Building and Jalan Tuanku Abdul Rahman, with Dataran Merdeka next.

Results: snatch theft down 57.6% in 2025; police efficiency up about 50%; about 50 cases solved with the cameras’ help. RM500 million invested since 2020 (The Rakyat Post; New Straits Times).

Going digital: 170 DBKL services online, all applications online by 2030 (Malay Mail).

Budget 2027: RM1.9 million for a Hybrid Command Centre to monitor land, green areas and public assets (MOF).

The next gap: connecting the data. JPS has flood data, DBKL has traffic and planning data, transport operators have commuter data. They are still islands. Using the cameras for traffic management is being discussed with the Transport Ministry (The Star).

Community and social care

Warga emas: RM4.5 million to turn Anjung Kelana, Taman Desa and Pasar Sementara Cheras into elderly day centres, so warga emas can stay active and connected (DBKL).

Urban communities: Budget 2027 extends Komuniti Angkat MADANI (RM50 million) to urban communities, including PPR, with skills courses from universities and TVET institutions (MOF).

Next focus: people experiencing homelessness. That is where we are focusing next.

Residents have a real voice: there is no elected council, but TTDI residents took DBKL to court over Taman Rimba Kiara and won at the Court of Appeal in 2021 (Thomas Philip Advocates & Solicitors).

My measure: KL is not defined by its skyline. It is defined by its people, and the parks, homes and voice we protect for them.
